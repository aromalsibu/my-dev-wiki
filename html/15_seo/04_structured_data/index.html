    Structured Data

    1. Overview

    Definition

    Structured data is a standardized format for providing explicit, machine-readable information about a web page and classifying its content. It uses a shared vocabulary — most commonly Schema.org — to label entities, relationships, and properties in a way that search engines, AI systems, and other machines can parse without ambiguity.

    <script type="application/ld+json">
{
    "@context": "https://schema.org",
    "@type": "Article",
    "headline": "How SVG Embedding Works",
    "image": "https://devnotes.example.com/og/svg-embedding.png",
    "datePublished": "2026-01-15T08:00:00Z",
    "dateModified": "2026-01-20T10:00:00Z",
    "author": {
        "@type": "Person",
        "name": "Jane Developer",
        "url": "https://devnotes.example.com/authors/jane"
    },
    "publisher": {
        "@type": "Organization",
        "name": "Dev Notes",
        "logo": {
            "@type": "ImageObject",
            "url": "https://devnotes.example.com/logo.png"
        }
    }
}
</script>

    Unlike the visible text of a page, which a machine must infer meaning from through natural language processing, structured data declares meaning explicitly. It says: "This is an Article. Its headline is X. Its author is a Person named Y. It was published on date Z." There is no ambiguity.

    The dominant vocabulary is Schema.org, a collaborative project founded by Google, Microsoft, Yahoo, and Yandex in 2011. It defines over 823 types — from Article and Product to LocalBusiness and Event — each with its own properties. Google supports a subset of these types for rich results, the enhanced search listings that show star ratings, prices, breadcrumbs, and other visual enhancements.

    Purpose

    Structured data serves several essential purposes in 2026:

    • Rich results — enables enhanced search listings: star ratings, product prices, breadcrumbs, event dates, recipe times, and more.
    • Search engine comprehension — helps Google understand what a page is about, not just what words it contains.
    • AI search visibility — makes content machine-readable for AI systems; most AI-cited pages (65% in AI Mode, 71% in ChatGPT) use schema markup.
    • Entity disambiguation — clearly identifies entities (people, organizations, products) so search engines and AI systems can distinguish them from similarly named entities.
    • Trust signals — Google's AI reads schema to verify claims, map entity relationships, and judge credibility — even when no rich result appears.
    • Product discovery — complete Product schema is how products become machine-readable and recommendable by AI shopping agents.
    • Voice search and assistants — structured data helps content appear in voice search, Google Maps, and other special features.
    • Content classification — helps search engines interpret and classify content, increasing the chances it is presented correctly in search results.

    In 2026, structured data is no longer an SEO bonus — it is a baseline requirement for discovery. With 25.11% of Google searches triggering an AI Overview in Q1 2026, and AI search visitors being 4.4× as valuable as traditional organic visitors, markup that makes a page easy to interpret protects access to a high-value audience.

    Where It Fits

    SEO Stack
│
├── Semantic Structure    ← document outline (headings, landmarks)
├── Heading Hierarchy     ← navigable tree within the structure
├── Metadata              ← title, description, canonical, robots
├── Structured Data       ← entity-level metadata (JSON-LD, Schema.org)
└── Content Quality       ← E-E-A-T, depth, originality

    Structured data is the entity layer of SEO — it sits on top of semantic structure and metadata, providing machine-readable descriptions of the entities and relationships on a page. It is the layer that tells search engines and AI systems not just what the page is about, but what things it describes and how they relate.

    2. Why It Exists

    The Problem

    A search engine cannot "see" a page. It cannot glance at a product listing and instantly know "this is a product, its price is $139.99, it is in stock, and it has 4.5 stars from 127 reviews." It must infer this from the visible text — and inference is unreliable.

    The visible text might say: "The Nike Air Zoom Pegasus 41 is available now for $139.99. Customers love it — 4.5 stars from 127 reviews." A human reads this and understands. A machine reads this and must use natural language processing to extract: product name, price, rating, review count. It might succeed. It might fail. It might extract the wrong number.

    Structured data removes the guesswork. It declares: "This is a Product. Its name is 'Nike Air Zoom Pegasus 41'. Its offers is an Offer with price 139.99 and priceCurrency USD. Its aggregateRating is 4.5 from 127 reviews."

    Previous Limitations

    Limitation Consequence
    No machine-readable metadata Search engines guessed content from text
    Ambiguous entities "Apple" the fruit vs. "Apple" the company
    No rich results Plain blue links only; no star ratings, prices, or breadcrumbs
    No entity relationships People, organizations, and products not connected
    No AI comprehension AI systems could not extract structured facts reliably
    Inconsistent markup formats Microdata and RDFa were difficult to maintain

    Why It Was Introduced

    Schema.org was launched in 2011 by Google, Microsoft, Yahoo, and Yandex as a unified vocabulary for structured data. Before Schema.org, search engines used competing vocabularies (Microformats, RDFa, Microdata), creating fragmentation. Schema.org consolidated them into a single standard.

    The format evolved:

    • 2011 — Schema.org launched with a core vocabulary.
    • 2015 — Google introduced Rich Results and the Rich Results Test.
    • 2019 — Google began recommending JSON-LD as the preferred format.
    • 2020s — AI systems began consuming structured data for answer generation.
    • 2023 — Google deprecated HowTo rich results (September 2023) and restricted FAQ rich results to government/health sites (August 2023).
    • 2025 — Google retired CourseInfo, EstimatedSalary, LearningVideo, and ClaimReview rich results (June 2025).
    • 2026 — Google fully retired FAQ rich results (May 7, 2026); Practice Problem support removed (January 6, 2026); no special schema required for AI Overviews (Google's May 2026 guidance).

    Structured data exists because machines need explicit, unambiguous instructions about what a page contains. It is the difference between a human reading a product description and a machine parsing a product feed.

    3. Syntax / Basic Usage

    The JSON-LD Format

    Google recommends JSON-LD (JavaScript Object Notation for Linked Data) as the preferred format for structured data. It is placed inside a <script type="application/ld+json"> block, separate from the visible HTML, and is the most robust and maintainable format.

    <script type="application/ld+json">
{
    "@context": "https://schema.org",
    "@type": "Article",
    "headline": "How SVG Embedding Works",
    "datePublished": "2026-01-15",
    "author": {
        "@type": "Person",
        "name": "Jane Developer"
    }
}
</script>

    Code Breakdown

    Property Purpose Required?
    @context The Schema.org vocabulary namespace ✅ Always required
    @type The entity type (Article, Product, etc.) ✅ Always required
    headline The article title ✅ For Article
    datePublished Publication date (ISO 8601) ✅ For Article
    author The author entity ✅ For Article

    The Minimum Viable Example

    <script type="application/ld+json">
{
    "@context": "https://schema.org",
    "@type": "Organization",
    "name": "Dev Notes",
    "url": "https://devnotes.example.com"
}
</script>

    Code Breakdown

    • @context — declares the Schema.org vocabulary.
    • @type — declares this as an Organization.
    • name — the organization's name.
    • url — the canonical URL of the organization.

    This is the simplest useful structured data block. It tells search engines "this page is about an organization called Dev Notes with this URL."

    A Complete Article Example

    <script type="application/ld+json">
{
    "@context": "https://schema.org",
    "@type": "Article",
    "headline": "How SVG Embedding Works",
    "description": "A complete guide to embedding SVG in HTML.",
    "image": {
        "@type": "ImageObject",
        "url": "https://devnotes.example.com/og/svg-embedding.png",
        "width": 1200,
        "height": 630
    },
    "datePublished": "2026-01-15T08:00:00Z",
    "dateModified": "2026-01-20T10:00:00Z",
    "author": {
        "@type": "Person",
        "name": "Jane Developer",
        "url": "https://devnotes.example.com/authors/jane",
        "sameAs": [
            "https://twitter.com/janedev",
            "https://linkedin.com/in/janedev"
        ]
    },
    "publisher": {
        "@type": "Organization",
        "name": "Dev Notes",
        "logo": {
            "@type": "ImageObject",
            "url": "https://devnotes.example.com/logo.png"
        }
    },
    "mainEntityOfPage": {
        "@type": "WebPage",
        "@id": "https://devnotes.example.com/svg-embedding"
    }
}
</script>

    Code Breakdown

    Property Purpose
    headline The article title (max 110 characters)
    description A short summary
    image The representative image (used for thumbnails)
    datePublished Publication date (ISO 8601 format)
    dateModified Last modification date
    author The author — a Person with name, url, and sameAs
    publisher The publishing organization with logo
    mainEntityOfPage The canonical URL of the page

    The Three Format Options

    Structured data can be expressed in three formats:

    Format Syntax Recommendation
    JSON-LD <script type="application/ld+json"> ✅ Google's stated preference
    Microdata itemscope, itemprop attributes in HTML ⚠️ Older; harder to maintain
    RDFa typeof, property attributes in HTML ⚠️ Older; less common

    Always use JSON-LD. It is separate from the visible HTML, easy to maintain, and the format that LLMs process most easily.

    The following types are actively supported by Google and eligible for rich results:

    4. Mental Model

    The "Product Feed" Mental Model

    Think of structured data as a product feed for your entire page. Just as an e-commerce store sends a product feed to Google Merchant Center — listing every product, price, and availability — structured data sends a feed to search engines and AI systems about every entity on your page: the article, its author, the organization that published it, the product it describes.

    The feed is not the product. It is the metadata about the product. But without the feed, the machine cannot reliably identify the product.

    The "Trust Signal, Not Display Trigger" Mental Model

    In 2026, structured data changed jobs. It used to be a display trigger — you added it to get stars or accordions in search results. Now it is a trust signal.

    Google's AI reads your schema to:

    • Verify your claims.
    • Map how your entities relate.
    • Judge whether you are a credible source.

    Even when no rich result ever appears on screen. Properly marked-up content is measurably more likely to be cited in AI answers. The 2026 skill is not adding more schema — it is keeping it honest and accurate.

    The "Retrievability, Not Citation" Mental Model

    Structured data is a retrievability instrument, not a citation strategy:

    Retrievability: Can the AI system find and parse your passage?
    └── Fixed by: clean schema, accurate entity data, JSON-LD.

Citation: Does the AI system trust your page enough to cite it?
    └── Fixed by: earned media, authority, brand mentions.

    A matched Ahrefs study of 1,885 pages found JSON-LD structured data produced no measurable AI citation lift in AI Mode or ChatGPT, and a slight decline in AI Overviews. However, 68% of AI-cited pages have structured data — double the web average. The schema types do not predict citation volume; content quality and structure matter more than the markup.

    The "Entity Graph" Mental Model

    Structured data creates an entity graph — a network of interconnected entities:

    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              ARTICLE                         │
│  headline: "How SVG Embedding Works"         │
│  datePublished: 2026-01-15                   │
│                                              │
│  ┌─────────────┐    ┌─────────────────────┐ │
│  │  AUTHOR     │    │  PUBLISHER          │ │
│  │  Person     │    │  Organization       │ │
│  │  Jane Dev   │    │  Dev Notes          │ │
│  │  sameAs:    │    │  logo: ...          │ │
│  │  [Twitter,  │    │                     │ │
│  │   LinkedIn] │    │                     │ │
│  └─────────────┘    └─────────────────────┘ │
│                                              │
│  mainEntityOfPage: WebPage                   │
│  @id: https://devnotes.example.com/...       │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘

    Each entity has a @type, a set of properties, and relationships to other entities. Search engines and AI systems traverse this graph to understand context, authority, and relevance.

    The "Minimal Viable Schema" Mental Model

    Not every page needs every property. Start with the minimum viable schema for the type, then add recommended properties as needed:

    Minimum viable Organization:
  @type, name, url

Minimum viable Article:
  @type, headline, datePublished

Minimum viable Product:
  @type, name, image, offers

Minimum viable LocalBusiness:
  @type, name, address, telephone

    A minimal valid schema is better than no schema. A complete schema is better than a minimal one. But an incorrect schema is worse than no schema — it feeds wrong facts to machines.

    5. Core Concepts

    Concept 1: Schema.org — The Vocabulary

    Schema.org is a collaborative vocabulary for structured data, founded by Google, Microsoft, Yahoo, and Yandex in 2011. It defines over 823 types and thousands of properties. Google supports a subset of these types for rich results.

    Concept 2: JSON-LD — The Format

    JSON-LD (JavaScript Object Notation for Linked Data) is Google's recommended format. It is placed in a <script type="application/ld+json"> block and does not affect the visible page.

    Concept 3: The @context and @type Properties

    Every JSON-LD block must include @context (the Schema.org vocabulary namespace) and @type (the entity type). These are the only universally required properties.

    Concept 4: Rich Results

    Rich results are enhanced search listings that display structured data — star ratings, prices, breadcrumbs, event dates, recipe times, and more. Rich results increase click-through rates compared to plain blue links.

    Concept 5: Active Schema Types (2026)

    Google still supports approximately 30 schema types that earn rich results: Product, Review, Article, Breadcrumb, Video, Event, Recipe, LocalBusiness, Organization, and others. The workhorses are all fine.

    Concept 6: Deprecated Schema Types

    FAQ rich results were retired on May 7, 2026. HowTo rich results were removed in September 2023. Other retired types include CourseInfo, EstimatedSalary, LearningVideo, ClaimReview, VehicleListing, Practice Problem, and Book Actions.

    Concept 7: No Special Schema for AI Overviews

    Google's May 2026 guidance states plainly: structured data is not required for AI Overviews or AI Mode. There is no special schema markup that makes you AI-visible. Standard crawling and indexing best practices are sufficient.

    Concept 8: Structured Data and AI Citations

    A matched Ahrefs study of 1,885 pages found JSON-LD produced no measurable AI citation lift. However, 68% of AI-cited pages have structured data — double the web average. The correlation is real, but the causation is not proven.

    Concept 9: Schema Drift

    Schema drift occurs when structured data falls out of sync with the visible page. A price changes in the CMS but the hardcoded JSON-LD still shows the old value. An author leaves but the author property still names them. You are now actively feeding wrong facts about yourself to AI systems.

    Concept 10: The "Mark Up Only What's on the Page" Rule

    The golden rule: mark up only what is genuinely on the page. Do not add FAQ schema to pages with no FAQ. Do not add Product schema to pages with no product. Markup that describes things not on the page is exactly what Google is cleaning up.

    Concept 11: The sameAs Property

    sameAs links your entity to its official profiles on other platforms — LinkedIn, Twitter, Wikipedia, Google Business Profile. This helps search engines and AI systems verify identity and build the entity graph.

    Concept 12: The @id Property

    @id is a unique identifier for an entity. It allows other pages to reference your entity consistently. Without @id, other pages cannot disambiguate your entity from similarly named entities.

    Concept 13: Absolute URLs

    Always use absolute URLs (with https://) in structured data. Relative URLs may not be resolved correctly by all parsers.

    Concept 14: ISO 8601 Date Format

    Dates in structured data must use ISO 8601 format: 2026-01-15 or 2026-01-15T08:00:00Z. Invalid date formats invalidate the structured data.

    Concept 15: Server-Rendered JSON-LD

    Per Google's December 2025 JavaScript SEO guidance, structured data injected via JavaScript may face delayed processing. For time-sensitive markup (especially Product and Offer), include JSON-LD in the initial server-rendered HTML.

    Concept 16: Validation Tools

    Use three tools to validate structured data:

    • Google Rich Results Test — search.google.com/test/rich-results — validates rich result eligibility.
    • Schema.org Validator — validator.schema.org — validates any Schema.org markup.
    • Bing Markup Validator — validates for Bing.

    Concept 17: The Entity Graph and Knowledge Graph

    Structured data feeds into Google's Knowledge Graph — the database of entities and relationships that powers knowledge panels and AI answers. A well-formed entity graph with @id, sameAs, and nested entities improves visibility.

    Concept 18: Product Schema and AI Shopping Agents

    Complete Product schema (price, availability, specifications, returns policy) is how products become machine-readable and recommendable by AI shopping agents (ChatGPT shopping, Google AI product discovery). Incomplete product schema means incomplete visibility.

    Concept 19: LocalBusiness Schema

    LocalBusiness is the most important type for businesses with physical locations. Required fields: @type, name, address (with street, city, postal code, country), telephone, url, @id. Fields that improve verifiability: taxID, sameAs, geo, openingHoursSpecification.

    Concept 20: The "Two Formats Retired" Clarification

    Google killed FAQ rich results in May 2026. Half of LinkedIn declared structured data dead. They read one headline and missed the actual story. Two formats retired — not the whole system. Google still supports around 30 schema types. FAQ didn't die because schema is bad — it died because it got abused.

    7. Internal Architecture / Under the Hood

    The Entity Graph and Linked Data

    JSON-LD is built on Linked Data principles. Each entity has a unique @id that can be referenced from other pages. The @context maps JSON keys to Schema.org URIs. The result is a graph of interconnected entities that spans the web.

    Article (@id: /svg-embedding)
├── author → Person (@id: /authors/jane)
│   └── sameAs → [Twitter, LinkedIn]
└── publisher → Organization (@id: /#organization)
    └── sameAs → [Wikipedia, LinkedIn]

    Schema.org Type Hierarchy

    Schema.org types form a hierarchy. All types inherit from Thing:

    Thing
├── CreativeWork
│   └── Article
│       ├── BlogPosting
│       └── NewsArticle
├── Product
│   └── ProductGroup
├── Organization
│   └── LocalBusiness
└── Person

    When you use a specific type (e.g., BlogPosting), you inherit all properties of Article and CreativeWork.

    JSON-LD parsed
    │
    ▼

    │
    ▼
Rich result eligibility rules applied:
    ├── Required properties present?
    ├── Recommended properties present?
    ├── Type supported for rich results?
    └── Content matches markup?
    │
    ▼
Rich result candidate → SERP

    Server-Rendered vs. JavaScript-Injected

    Google's December 2025 guidance: structured data injected via JavaScript may face delayed processing. For time-sensitive markup (especially Product and Offer), include JSON-LD in the initial server-rendered HTML.

    Server-rendered JSON-LD:
  ├── Available immediately to crawler
  ├── No JavaScript execution required
  └── Preferred for Product, Offer, Event

JavaScript-injected JSON-LD:
  ├── May face delayed processing
  ├── Requires JavaScript rendering
  └── Acceptable for non-time-sensitive types

    "@type": "Article",

    11. Best Practices

    1. Use JSON-LD as the primary format. Why: Google's stated preference; LLMs process it most easily.
    2. Mark up only what is genuinely on the page. Why: markup that describes things not on the page is exactly what Google is cleaning up.
    3. Populate every property the type supports and the page genuinely contains. Why: complete schema improves verifiability.
    4. Use absolute URLs with https://. Why: relative URLs may not resolve correctly.
    5. Use ISO 8601 date format. Why: invalid date formats invalidate structured data.
    6. Include @id for entities. Why: allows other pages to reference your entity consistently.
    7. Use sameAs to link to official profiles. Why: helps verify identity and build the entity graph.
    8. Server-render JSON-LD for time-sensitive types (Product, Offer). Why: JavaScript-injected schema may face delayed processing.
    9. Keep schema in sync with visible content. Why: schema drift feeds wrong facts to machines.
    10. Validate with Rich Results Test, Schema.org Validator, and Bing Markup Validator. Why: different tools catch different errors.
    11. Check for deprecated types before implementing. Why: FAQ, HowTo, and others no longer earn rich results.
    12. Do not add special schema for AI Overviews. Why: Google's May 2026 guidance is explicit — no special markup is required.
    13. Do not implement llms.txt. Why: Google explicitly says you don't need machine-readable files for AI search.
    14. Do not chunk content artificially. Why: Google's mythbusting guide calls it unnecessary.
    15. Focus on content quality and structure, not markup. Why: signals that drive AI citations are content quality, topical authority, original data, and well-structured Q&A format.
    16. Audit schema quarterly. Why: content changes, prices change, authors change.
    17. Use Organization schema on the homepage. Why: defines the brand entity for the entire site.
    18. Use BreadcrumbList on every page. Why: helps users and machines understand page hierarchy.
    19. Nest entities correctly. Why: nested entities (author within article, publisher within article) create a richer graph.
    20. Test with real users and monitor Search Console. Why: rich result errors appear in Search Console.

    12. Common Mistakes

    ❌ Mistake: Schema Drift

    <!-- CMS price: $129.99 -->
<!-- JSON-LD price: $139.99 -->
"price": "139.99"

    Why it's wrong: You are actively feeding wrong facts about yourself to AI systems.

    ✅ Correct: Keep schema in sync with visible content.

    ❌ Mistake: Marking Up Things Not on the Page

    <!-- No FAQ on the page, but FAQPage schema is present -->
<script type="application/ld+json">
{ "@type": "FAQPage", ... }
</script>

    Why it's wrong: Markup that describes things not on the page is what Google is cleaning up.

    ✅ Correct: Mark up only what is genuinely on the page.

    ❌ Mistake: Using Deprecated Types

    <!-- FAQPage rich results were retired May 7, 2026 -->
<script type="application/ld+json">
{ "@type": "FAQPage", ... }
</script>

    Why it's wrong: No rich result benefit; wasted effort.

    ✅ Correct: Remove FAQPage schema or keep it without expecting rich results.

    ❌ Mistake: Relative URLs

    "url": "/svg-embedding"

    Why it's wrong: Relative URLs may not resolve correctly.

    ✅ Correct:

    "url": "https://devnotes.example.com/svg-embedding"

    ❌ Mistake: Invalid Date Format

    "datePublished": "January 15, 2026"

    Why it's wrong: Invalid date format invalidates structured data.

    ✅ Correct:

    "datePublished": "2026-01-15"

    ❌ Mistake: Missing @context

    <script type="application/ld+json">
{
    "@type": "Article",
    "headline": "..."
}
</script>

    Why it's wrong: Without @context, the parser does not know which vocabulary to use.

    ✅ Correct:

    <script type="application/ld+json">
{
    "@context": "https://schema.org",
    "@type": "Article",
    "headline": "..."
}
</script>

    ❌ Mistake: Implementing FAQ Schema for AI Citations

    <script type="application/ld+json">
{ "@type": "FAQPage", ... }
</script>

    Why it's wrong: A matched Ahrefs study of 1,885 pages found JSON-LD produced no measurable AI citation lift.

    ✅ Correct: Keep FAQ content in your content; the Q&A format is valuable, but do not treat FAQPage schema as an AI search lever.

    ❌ Mistake: Keyword Stuffing in Schema

    "name": "SEO, SEO tools, SEO services, best SEO"

    Why it's wrong: Degrades readability and can be treated as spam.

    ✅ Correct:

    "name": "How to Choose the Right SEO Tool for Your Team"

    ❌ Mistake: No Validation

    <!-- Never validated with Rich Results Test -->

    Why it's wrong: Errors go undetected.

    ✅ Correct: Always validate with Rich Results Test and Schema.org Validator.

    13. Performance Considerations

    • JSON-LD has no runtime cost — it is a script block parsed by crawlers, not by users.
    • Structured data adds minimal bytes — typically under 2 KB uncompressed.
    • Server-rendered JSON-LD is preferred for time-sensitive types.
    • Validation tools are lightweight and run in the browser.
    • CI/CD integration adds seconds to pipeline but catches regressions.

    Performance Tips

    1. Keep JSON-LD blocks concise — include only relevant properties.
    2. Server-render JSON-LD for Product and Offer types.
    3. Validate in CI/CD with a simple script.
    4. Use the Rich Results Test before deploying.

    14. Security Considerations

    Schema Injection

    If structured data is generated from user input, escape it. JSON-LD injection can break the script block or inject malicious JSON.

    Schema Drift as a Security Risk

    Feeding wrong facts about yourself to AI systems is a trust risk. Inaccurate schema can damage credibility and, in extreme cases, be exploited by competitors.

    Fake Signals

    Self-reviews, FAQ stuffing, and markup that describes things not actually on the page are spam vectors. Google is actively cleaning these up.

    Entity Hijacking

    If your @id is not unique or your sameAs links are incorrect, another entity could claim your identity in the Knowledge Graph.

    Validation as Defense

    Regular validation with Rich Results Test and Schema.org Validator catches errors before they reach production.
